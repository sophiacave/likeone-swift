Flip Each Card: Mistake → Fix

❌ Mistake 1: Being Too Vague "Help me with my resume." Why is this bad?
✅ THE FIX: "Rewrite the experience section of my resume for a senior product manager role at a fintech company. Here is my current version: [paste]" Vague prompts = vague output. The AI does not know which part, what role, or what help means.
❌ Mistake 2: Multiple Questions at Once "What is the best CRM, how do I set it up, and can you write me a sales email and suggest a pricing strategy?" Why is this bad?
✅ THE FIX: "I am setting up a CRM for my 5-person sales team selling B2B SaaS ($5K-$50K deals). Compare HubSpot, Pipedrive, and Close.io in a table: price, best for, biggest limitation." One focused question gets 10x the depth of four bundled ones.
❌ Mistake 3: No Audience Specified "Explain machine learning." Why is this bad?
✅ THE FIX: "Explain machine learning to a marketing manager who has never written code. Use analogies from advertising and customer behavior. Under 200 words." Explain X to a PhD and a teenager are completely different tasks.
❌ Mistake 4: Accepting First Output Getting mediocre output → "AI sucks" → giving up Why is this bad?
✅ THE FIX: Iterate! "Make it more specific to my industry. Remove the generic advice. Give me numbers." First outputs are first drafts. The iteration is where quality lives. Use the 4 follow-up patterns from Lesson 7.
❌ Mistake 5: Being Polite Instead of Clear "If it is not too much trouble, could you maybe try to perhaps write something about marketing? Only if you have time!" Why is this bad?
✅ THE FIX: "Write 5 email subject lines for a Black Friday sale targeting existing customers. Product: online courses. Tone: urgent but not spammy." AI does not have feelings. Hedging adds noise and reduces clarity. Be direct.
❌ Mistake 6: No Examples of Good Trying to match a specific style without showing the AI what it looks like Why is this bad?
✅ THE FIX: Paste a sample. "Write in a style similar to this: [example paragraph]." Even a 2-sentence example gives AI a target. Without one, it is guessing in the dark about your voice and style preferences.
❌ Mistake 7: Treating AI Like a Search Engine "What is content marketing?" Why is this bad?
✅ THE FIX: "Create a 30-day content marketing plan for a new yoga studio targeting busy professionals. Include specific post ideas, platforms, and a posting schedule." AI generates, analyzes, transforms, and creates. Asking what is X uses 5% of its capability.
❌ Mistake 8: Ignoring What You Already Know Not sharing your drafts, data, or existing knowledge with the AI Why is this bad?
✅ THE FIX: You have a rough draft? PASTE IT. You have data? INCLUDE IT. You have a style guide? SHARE IT. AI works best building on something you started — not creating from nothing. Your existing knowledge is the most valuable context you can provide.

Good Practice or Common Mistake?

1You write: "Make it better." Is this a good prompting practice?

Explanation: "Make it better" gives Claude no direction. Better: "Make this paragraph more concise, use active voice, and cut it to 2 sentences."

2Which of these is a strong prompting habit?

Explanation: Iteration is the single most powerful prompting technique. First outputs are drafts — the magic happens when you refine.

3You want Claude to write a professional email. What should you include?

Explanation: Context is everything. Role + audience + tone + key points gives Claude exactly what it needs to write something you can actually send.

4Your prompt failed. What should you try first?

Explanation: When a prompt fails, diagnose what was missing: specificity, context, examples, or format. One targeted fix usually solves it.

5Which technique immediately improves Claude output quality?

Explanation: Pasting examples (few-shot prompting) shows Claude exactly what you want. It learns your style, format, and quality bar from real examples.

Anti-Patterns

5 habits that secretly sabotage your prompts.

Beyond the 8 big mistakes, there are subtler habits that erode prompt quality over time. These are the ones even intermediate AI users fall into.

Anti-Patterns — Flip to See the Fix

🔁 Anti-Pattern 1: Copy-Paste Laziness Using the exact same prompt for every task without adapting it to the situation.
THE FIX: Templates are starting points, not finished products. Before sending any template, change at least 3 variables: audience, tone, and one specific detail about THIS task. A prompt that worked for a blog post will fail for a sales email — even if the topic is the same.
📏 Anti-Pattern 2: Length Worship Believing longer prompts are always better prompts.
THE FIX: Length without specificity is just noise. A 20-word prompt with a clear audience, format, and constraint beats a 200-word prompt full of vague instructions. The goal is PRECISION, not word count. Every sentence in your prompt should eliminate a guess the AI would otherwise make.
🎯 Anti-Pattern 3: Moving the Goalposts Changing what you want in every follow-up instead of building on what worked.
THE FIX: Before iterating, identify what IS working. Then target your follow-up at the specific thing that is not. BAD: "No, actually make it completely different." GOOD: "Keep the structure and examples. Rewrite the intro to be more direct and cut the conclusion by half."
🤖 Anti-Pattern 4: Prompt Stuffing Adding every RCFCE component to every prompt regardless of whether it helps.
THE FIX: Match the prompt complexity to the task complexity. Simple task ("Translate this to Spanish"): Context + Format is enough. Complex task ("Write a board presentation"): All five RCFCE components. Over-specifying a simple task wastes your time and can actually confuse the output.
⏳ Anti-Pattern 5: The Sunk Cost Spiral Continuing to iterate on a fundamentally broken prompt because you have already spent 10 minutes on it.
THE FIX: The 3-round rule. If 3 rounds of iteration have not moved you significantly closer to what you want, the original prompt has a structural problem. Start fresh. Analyze WHY it failed: Missing context? Wrong role? Bad format choice? Fix THAT in your new prompt.
Corrections Lab

Real mistakes, real fixes — practice your diagnostic eye.

For each broken prompt below, identify the mistake and read the corrected version. This is the skill that separates good AI users from great ones — the ability to diagnose and fix a prompt in seconds.

Correction 1 — The Kitchen Sink Prompt
BROKEN:
"I need a business plan, market analysis, financial projections,
marketing strategy, competitive landscape, team structure, and
a pitch deck outline for my new app idea."

DIAGNOSIS: 7 asks in one prompt. Each one deserves its own
focused conversation. The output will be shallow on everything.

FIXED:
"I am building a meal-planning app for busy parents. Start with
a competitive landscape: identify the top 5 competitors, their
pricing, and one gap each of them has. Format as a table with
columns: App Name, Monthly Price, Target User, Key Gap."

Then follow up for each subsequent section in separate prompts.
Correction 2 — The Invisible Audience
BROKEN:
"Explain blockchain technology and its potential impact on
the financial services industry."

DIAGNOSIS: No audience. This could be for a 10-year-old or a
bank CTO. The AI will default to Wikipedia-level generic.

FIXED:
"Explain blockchain to a traditional bank's risk management
team. They understand finance but not distributed systems.
Focus on 3 specific impacts: settlement speed, fraud reduction,
and regulatory compliance. For each, give one real-world example
from a bank that has implemented it. Under 400 words."
Correction 3 — The Tone-Deaf Prompt
BROKEN:
"Write a message to my team about the layoffs."

DIAGNOSIS: No tone, no context about severity, no guidance on
what to emphasize. The AI could write something callous or
something so soft it buries the message.

FIXED:
"Write a message from a VP of Engineering to a 40-person team.
We are laying off 6 people next week (they have been notified
separately). Tone: honest, empathetic, and direct — do not
sugarcoat but do not be cold. Address: why this happened
(revenue miss), who is affected (roles, not names), what
support is available (severance, job placement), and what
the remaining team should expect. Under 300 words."
Quick Diagnostic

When a prompt fails, ask yourself these 3 questions.

Diagnose These Prompts

1A prompt says: "Write something about our product for the website." What is the PRIMARY mistake?

Explanation: Something, product, and website are all undefined. The AI has to guess everything. The fix: specify exactly what content, for which product, for which page, for which audience.

2You asked AI to write a blog post. The content is solid but full of buzzwords like leverage, utilize, and synergy. What should your follow-up be?

Explanation: This is a perfect case for a negative constraint follow-up. The content structure is good — you just need to eliminate the AI-isms. One follow-up fixes it.

3Your prompt has great context and format but the output reads like a textbook. What is the most likely missing element?

Explanation: A tone or audience constraint would fix this: Conversational, not academic. Write like you are explaining to a friend, not lecturing. Negative constraints are the fastest way to eliminate unwanted tone.
Flowchart

The 60-second prompt diagnostic.

When a prompt fails, run through this mental checklist in order. Stop at the first issue you find — that's likely the root cause.

Step 1: Did I specify WHO the audience is? If no, add it. Audience changes everything.
Step 2: Did I specify WHAT format I want? If I just said "help me with X," I gave the AI no shape to work with.
Step 3: Am I asking ONE clear thing or bundling multiple requests? Split them if you bundled.
Step 4: Does the output sound generic? Add a negative constraint banning the specific AI-isms you see.
Step 5: Did I share what I already have? Paste your draft, data, examples, or style reference. AI builds on something faster than it creates from nothing.

Most failed prompts are fixed by steps 1-3. If you're still struggling after all five, the task might need to be broken into smaller pieces — one prompt per piece.
